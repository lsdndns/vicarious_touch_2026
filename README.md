# Abstract
At the neural level, the ability to engage with others’ tactile experiences is thought to be supported by mirror mechanisms, also known as somatosensory resonance: when observing social touch, the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices exhibit activations similar to those observed when we are being touched ourselves. This somatosensory resonance was found to be enhanced in social contexts. However, along with variations of the types of stimuli and tasks used, previous works have also not systematically included both controls on the specificity of human interactions (non-social touch) and on the specificity of tactile interactions (social no-touch). In this study, we aimed to do so using a novel EEG/ERP method that allows dissociating visually driven somatosensory activity from carryover arising in visual areas. We expected that observing social and non-social touch would elicit somatosensory evoked potentials of higher amplitude than observing non-tactile social interactions (Hypothesis 1). Moreover, the observation of social touch was expected to elicit somatosensory evoked potentials of higher amplitude than the observation of non-social touch, reflecting the additional sensitivity of somatosensory cortices to observed social tactile interactions (Hypothesis 2). Contrary to our hypotheses and in contrast with most previous works, we observed no significant difference between the observation of either tactile condition and the no-touch condition, nor between the observation of the social and non-social touch conditions. These results suggest that somatosensory resonance does not rely solely on the visual perception of touch. Rather, other factors such as attention and inter-individual differences may come into play.
Keywords: Social touch, Vicarious touch, Somatosensory activations, Somatosensation, Social perception, EEG, ERP, SEP
